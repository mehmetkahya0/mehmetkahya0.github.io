Predicting F1 races with simulation, ML and real telemetry

Inside my F1 race predictor: a lap-by-lap stochastic simulator with tyre and weather models, a three-model scikit-learn ensemble, and FastF1 data blended into one prediction.

Formula 1 is a wonderful modelling problem. Car pace, driver skill, tyre wear, fuel load, weather, pit strategy and pure chance all interact over sixty-odd laps, and every Sunday reality checks your assumptions. My F1 Race Predictor is a Python project that tries to capture enough of that to make useful predictions, and to explain why it predicts what it does.

It combines three very different sources of signal: a lap-by-lap probabilistic race simulator, a small ensemble of machine-learning models, and real timing data pulled from the FastF1 library. This post walks through each layer and the honest limitations of each.

The data model

Everything starts with plain Python classes for drivers, teams and tracks, updated for the 2026 grid: eleven teams including the new Cadillac entry, twenty-two drivers and a twenty-four-race calendar. A driver is a bundle of ratings on a 1–100 scale: 

class Driver:
    def __init__(self, name, team, number, nationality, age, experience,
                 skill_wet, skill_dry, skill_overtaking, consistency, aggression,
                 tire_management=80, racecraft=80, qualifying_pace=80):
        ...

Teams carry car performance, reliability and pit-stop efficiency. The 2026 regulations add attributes such as energy_recovery for the bigger MGU-K and active_aero for the new movable aerodynamics. Tracks carry length, lap count, a tyre-wear rating and an energy-demand rating. Keeping this as plain data makes it easy to tweak a single number and immediately see its effect on a race.

Layer 1: a lap-by-lap race simulator

The heart of the project is RaceSimulator. Qualifying produces a grid, then the race is simulated lap by lap. Each driver's lap time is a sum of interpretable terms: 

lap_time = (base_time           # from track length
            + driver_time_delta # up to 2.5 s from driver skill
            + car_time_delta    # up to 2.0 s from car performance
            + weather_impact    # wet-weather skill matters a lot more in the rain
            + tire_impact       # grows with tyre age, track wear and tyre management
            + fuel_impact       # negative: cars get faster as fuel burns off
            + random_factor     # Gaussian noise, smaller for consistent drivers
           ) * track_modifier

Two details make the output feel realistic. First, randomness is controlled: the standard deviation of the noise shrinks with a driver's consistency rating, so consistent drivers produce tight lap times while erratic ones sometimes shine and sometimes fade. Second, the fuel term is negative and grows with the lap number, so stints naturally get faster before tyre wear takes over.

Because the simulation is stochastic, a single race is one sample. Run it many times and you get a distribution of outcomes, which is far more honest than a single "this driver wins" answer.

Tyres and strategy

Tyre behaviour lives in strategy.py. Each compound has a pace offset and a base degradation rate per lap:

CompoundPace vs softDegradation / lap
Softbaseline3.0%
Medium+0.4 s2.2%
Hard+0.8 s1.5%
Intermediate+2.0 s2.5%
Wet+3.0 s2.0%

The base rate is then multiplied by the track's wear rating, a temperature factor (hot tracks above 45 °C eat tyres 30% faster, cold ones below 15 °C 20% slower) and a weather factor. Slicks on a wet track degrade five times faster, and wet tyres on a dry track six times faster, which is how the model "decides" when a tyre choice is wrong. PitStopStrategy builds a plan from these numbers, including undercut and overcut opportunities, and pit stops take longer or shorter depending on the team's pit crew rating.

Weather and incidents

Weather is generated per track and month: dry conditions come with a 0–15% rain chance, mixed conditions with 40–80% and wet ones with 70–100%. Incidents add the chaos that makes F1 F1: mechanical failures (more likely when a team's combined reliability and energy-recovery score is low), collisions, penalties and energy-system problems. Without them, the fastest car would win every simulated race, which is not how the real sport works.

Layer 2: a three-model ML ensemble

The second opinion comes from ml_predictor.py: three scikit-learn regressors that predict a finishing position from features such as grid position, driver ratings, car performance, track characteristics and whether the race is wet. 

self.rf_model = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=100, max_depth=10, min_samples_split=5,
                                      min_samples_leaf=2, random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)
self.gb_model = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=100, max_depth=6, learning_rate=0.1,
                                          min_samples_split=5, random_state=42)
self.nn_model = MLPRegressor(hidden_layer_sizes=(64, 32, 16), activation='relu', solver='adam',
                             learning_rate='adaptive', max_iter=500, random_state=42,
                             early_stopping=True)

Predictions are blended with fixed weights (Random Forest 0.40, Gradient Boosting 0.35, neural network 0.25) and the spread between the three models becomes a confidence score: when they agree, confidence goes up. Each driver also gets a probability distribution over positions, modelled as a Gaussian around the ensemble prediction.

Honest caveat The models are trained on 5,000 synthetic samples generated from F1 domain rules: grid position as the strongest predictor, adjusted by car and driver performance, wet-weather skill and overtaking ability, plus Gaussian noise. That makes the ML layer a smooth, learned version of my own assumptions rather than an independent source of truth. Training on real historical results is the single biggest improvement on my list.

Layer 3: real data from FastF1

The third layer grounds everything in reality. real_data_integration.py uses the FastF1 library to load event schedules and recent race sessions, then derives driver and team form and track-specific data. FastF1's on-disk cache is enabled, so the first run is slow and every run after that is fast. Loading is wrapped in a terminal loading screen with FastF1's verbose logging suppressed, because a prediction tool shouldn't scroll hundreds of log lines past you.

Blending the three

The enhanced model combines everything with explicit weights:

REAL_DATA_WEIGHT = 0.50  # how much to trust recent real-world form
SIMULATION_WEIGHT = 0.25
ML_WEIGHT = 0.25

Real data gets the largest share because current form beats any static rating. The simulator and the ML ensemble each contribute a quarter, the simulator for race dynamics such as tyres, weather and incidents, and the ensemble for a smoothed statistical view.

How well does it work?

I tested the simulator against sixteen races of the 2025 season while developing it. The project README keeps the full table; the summary:

MetricResult
Winner predicted correctly56.3%
Average podium accuracy68.8%
Drivers within ±3 positions82.4%
Mean absolute error2.3 positions

It is strongest at street circuits like Monaco, where track characteristics dominate, and weakest where races were decided by things no model sees coming: first-lap collisions, a disqualification after the race, or a team finding pace that nobody expected. Sixteen races is a small sample, so I read these numbers as "clearly better than guessing", not as a precise accuracy figure.

Visualizing the race

Numbers alone are hard to trust, so the project produces matplotlib/seaborn charts: position changes across the race, lap-time progression, tyre-degradation curves, driver radar charts and team heatmaps. The position chart in particular is a great debugging tool. If a driver jumps ten places in one lap without a pit stop or an incident, the model has a bug.

What I would do next

  • Train on real history. Replace the synthetic training set with several seasons of FastF1 race results and evaluate with a proper time-based split, training on earlier races and testing on later ones.
  • Learn the blend weights. The 50/25/25 split is hand-tuned; fitting it on held-out races would be more principled.
  • Calibrated probabilities. Report win and podium probabilities from many simulation runs, and check their calibration against reality.

The project taught me that the hardest part of prediction is not the model but being honest about uncertainty. A good F1 predictor should be confident at Monaco and humble at Spa in the rain.

All predictions are for entertainment and educational purposes.
